\section*{Abstract}
Reconstructing spatial transcriptomics from limited molecular measurements requires accounting for tissue context and measurement placement. We introduce GraphPathology, a geometry-aware framework separating histological representations from reconstruction with observed molecular anchors. We investigated different forms of graph neural networks (GNNs) through matched graph and non-graph visual comparisons. In two breast-cancer cohorts comprising 25 evaluation patients, reconstruction from 20\% distributed molecular observations achieved median patient Pearson correlations of 0.359 and 0.416. Controlled comparisons in 22 previously evaluated patients held hidden targets, estimator and anchor count fixed. Excluding anchors from a central region reduced median paired correlation by 0.1541 and produced a median paired relative increase in mean squared error of 45.0\%; all patients deteriorated. A graph-based predictor improved anchor-assisted correlation and errors over its matched non-graph counterpart in all 22 patients with both visual backbones, but did not outperform molecular interpolation overall. These findings identify measurement placement as a determinant of reconstruction accuracy and distinguish contextual gains from the value of measured expression. Measuring one fifth of locations motivates potential location-dependent savings; total assay savings require prospective validation. Code: \url{https://github.com/HySonLab/GraphPathology}.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} spatial transcriptomics; sparse reconstruction; geometry-aware graph learning; measurement geometry; histopathology; breast cancer

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) connects molecular measurements to the organization of intact tissue, making it possible to study gene-expression patterns in their anatomical context~\cite{stahl2016}. This spatial perspective is particularly important in cancer, where malignant, stromal and immune populations occupy heterogeneous neighborhoods rather than forming a well-mixed system. Breast-cancer studies have used spatially resolved measurements to characterize regional transcriptional programs, tumor-associated cell populations and tissue architectures that are obscured when spatial relationships are lost~\cite{andersson2021,wu2021,bassiouni2023}. Reconstructing molecular profiles across tissue is therefore more than a prediction problem: it concerns which aspects of biological organization can be recovered from the measurements available at particular locations.

Despite these opportunities, practical ST studies must balance molecular coverage, spatial resolution, tissue coverage and experimental resources. Histological images provide a complementary source of information, motivating computational methods that predict expression from morphology or integrate images with measured transcriptomes~\cite{he2020,monjo2022,bergenstrahle2022,istar2024}. Paired resources such as HEST-1k further enable systematic investigation of expression--morphology relationships across samples and technologies~\cite{jaume2024}. These developments raise an important question for resource-constrained studies: how much information can be recovered when molecular profiles are measured at only a subset of tissue locations, and when does histological context add useful information beyond those measurements?

We address this question through a specific within-section reconstruction task. A predefined gene panel is observed at selected locations, termed \emph{molecular anchors}, and the objective is to estimate the same panel at the remaining locations. Coordinates and histology are available throughout the retained tissue, but the complete target-expression vector is withheld at every unobserved site. Sparsity here refers to the fraction of locations with available molecular measurements, not to zeros within a measured expression vector. The task consequently differs from denoising measured counts, predicting an entirely unassayed set of genes, or increasing the resolution of a molecular map. It also differs from image-only prediction because same-section molecular observations are explicitly available at inference.

Measurement coverage alone does not specify the difficulty of this task. Two observation sets with the same number of anchors can differ substantially in their proximity to the locations being reconstructed. Distributed measurements may provide nearby support throughout a section, whereas exclusion of measurements from a contiguous region forces reconstruction to rely on more distant anchors. Spatially structured evaluation is therefore essential when assessing predictions intended for spatially structured missingness~\cite{roberts2017}. However, a comparison between distributed and regional masks can also change the evaluated tissue and the reconstruction operator. To attribute an observed difference to anchor placement, the measurement count, hidden targets and estimator must instead be held fixed.

Graph representation learning provides a natural language for expressing these relationships. Tissue locations can be represented as nodes, while edges encode spatial neighborhoods or other relationships among their features. Established graph neural networks (GNNs) learn contextual representations through neighborhood aggregation and message passing~\cite{kipf2017,hamilton2017,gilmer2017}. Related ideas have been applied to general visual recognition, hierarchical histopathology analysis and spatial-transcriptomics analysis~\cite{han2022vig,pati2022hact,hu2021spagcn}. For sparse reconstruction, however, two uses of a graph must be distinguished: a graph that contextualizes histological features and a graph that transfers measured expression from available anchors. Their edges describe different information pathways, and a gain from access to molecular observations should not automatically be attributed to the image representation or graph architecture.

This distinction also determines the appropriate comparison. An image-only predictor must infer expression from relationships learned in other samples, whereas a molecular interpolator receives patient-specific expression from the section under evaluation. Comparing them assesses the value of those observations, but does not isolate the benefit of a particular learning algorithm. The incremental value of morphology is better assessed by supplying the same anchors to molecular-only and image--molecular predictors. Cross-study benchmarking has further shown the importance of evaluating generalization, data characteristics and downstream relevance rather than relying on a single prediction score~\cite{wang2025}. Accordingly, architecture, information access and measurement placement should be varied separately wherever possible.

Here, we introduce \emph{GraphPathology}, a geometry-aware reconstruction and evaluation framework that separates these factors. A coordinate-defined \emph{tissue-context graph} supports the representation of histological neighborhoods, while an observation-dependent \emph{molecular-support graph} connects unobserved sites to measured anchors. Molecular reconstruction, image prediction, fixed fusion and observed-residual interpolation are evaluated as distinct routes. The framework accommodates different forms of GNNs without requiring that learned image context improve every reconstruction setting. Its methodological contribution is the explicit separation of representation learning, permitted molecular information and observation geometry, together with controlled comparisons that identify which components account for predictive performance.

We evaluate a development-selected 50-gene panel in two independent breast-cancer studies comprising 25 original evaluation patients. Additional estimator, placement and neural comparisons reuse these previously evaluated cohorts and are interpreted as exploratory follow-up analyses. The evaluation combines distributed coverage, regional missingness, patient-resolved effects and checks that hidden expression cannot influence predictions. This design connects the practical motivation for sparse acquisition to a testable question about reconstruction reliability, without equating fewer retrospectively revealed locations with demonstrated savings in laboratory expenditure.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions and principal findings.}
The study makes four complementary contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An explicit two-graph reconstruction framework.} GraphPathology distinguishes histological context from observed molecular support and evaluates molecular-only, image-only and combined predictors under defined information constraints. This formulation enables comparisons that separate the contribution of measured anchors from that of learned spatial representations.
\item \textbf{A controlled test of measurement geometry.} With hidden targets, estimator and anchor count held fixed in 22 previously evaluated patients, excluding anchors from a central region reduced median paired Pearson correlation by 0.1541 and increased mean squared error by 45.0\% (median paired relative change) for inverse-distance interpolation at 20\% coverage. All 22 patients deteriorated; uniform interpolation showed the same direction.
\item \textbf{Multi-cohort evidence for sparse molecular reconstruction.} Using 20\% distributed observations, reconstruction achieved median patient correlations of 0.359 and 0.416 in the original three-patient Wu and 22-patient Bassiouni evaluations, respectively. Comparisons across three measurement budgets and twenty molecular estimators characterize the dependence on coverage and reconstruction rule without selecting an external-data winner.
\item \textbf{Matched evidence for context-dependent graph gains.} Within both frozen backbones, GCN-assisted reconstruction improved correlation and errors over matched local prediction in all 22 Bassiouni patients, while molecular interpolation remained stronger overall. Neighborhood-mean and shuffled controls delimit the interpretation of these gains.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Graph representation learning and geometric inductive biases.}
Graph convolutional networks combine node attributes with local graph structure, while GraphSAGE learns inductive representations by aggregating neighborhood features~\cite{kipf2017,hamilton2017}. The message-passing formulation provides a broader framework for relating these architectures through learned messages, aggregation and node updates~\cite{gilmer2017}. Subsequent developments address complementary representational requirements: GraphGPS combines local message passing with positional or structural encodings and global attention, whereas EGNNs use invariant messages and equivariant coordinate updates to respect Euclidean transformations~\cite{rampasek2022,satorras2021}. These principles motivate ways to represent tissue geometry, but architectural expressivity and symmetry do not by themselves establish useful molecular prediction. GraphPathology uses these established principles as controlled representation alternatives within a reconstruction framework whose distinguishing focus is the separation of learned context, available molecular measurements and anchor placement.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Relational and graph-based learning for general images.}
Visual representation learning also benefits from treating interactions among image regions explicitly. Non-local neural networks aggregate features across spatially separated positions, providing a mechanism for information exchange beyond a local convolutional neighborhood~\cite{wang2018nonlocal}. Vision GNN represents image patches as graph nodes and alternates graph-based feature aggregation with feature transformations for recognition and detection~\cite{han2022vig}. These approaches demonstrate that image structure need not be restricted to a regular convolutional grid and motivate examination of both local and nonlocal tissue context. They nevertheless optimize visual tasks rather than reconstruction from experimentally observed molecular anchors. GraphPathology differs by evaluating visual relations at registered tissue locations and testing whether their representations improve gene-expression reconstruction beyond the same measured molecular support.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Graph learning in computational histopathology.}
Graph-based pathology methods represent tissue through biologically interpretable entities or spatially arranged image patches. HACT-Net combines cell- and tissue-level graphs to model hierarchical tissue composition for histopathological classification~\cite{pati2022hact}. Patch-GCN represents whole-slide images as spatial patch graphs and aggregates their contextual features for patient-survival prediction~\cite{chen2021patchgcn}. Both illustrate how relationships among local structures can support tissue-level inference, although their node definitions, supervision and outputs differ. These studies motivate contextual histological representations without implying that improvements in classification or prognosis transfer directly to molecular reconstruction. In contrast, GraphPathology predicts a fixed expression panel at withheld spatial locations and evaluates histological context against a molecular-only predictor supplied with the identical same-section anchors.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Graph learning for spatial domains and tissue organization.}
Spatial-transcriptomics methods have used graphs to combine expression similarity, physical proximity and histological information. SpaGCN integrates gene expression, spatial coordinates and histology for spatial-domain identification and analysis of spatially variable genes~\cite{hu2021spagcn}. STAGATE learns low-dimensional representations with an adaptive graph-attention autoencoder, while GraphST uses graph-based self-supervised contrastive learning for spatial clustering, integration and deconvolution~\cite{dong2022stagate,long2023graphst}. SPACEL extends spatial analysis to cell-type deconvolution, domain identification and alignment of tissue sections~\cite{spacel2023}. These tasks establish the value of spatial relationships for interpreting measured molecular profiles. GraphPathology instead evaluates expression prediction at sites whose complete target vectors are unavailable, restricting molecular messages to observed anchors and explicitly testing the effect of their placement.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Graph-guided reconstruction of spatial expression.}
Graph structure has also been used directly for transcriptome reconstruction. GNTD models spatial expression as a tensor indexed by spatial coordinates and genes, combining nonlinear tensor decomposition with graph regularization informed by spatial neighborhoods and gene functional relationships~\cite{song2023gntd}. Its formulation demonstrates that both spatial and molecular relational priors can guide recovery from sparse expression data. Graph-guided completion therefore provides an important precedent for molecular reconstruction, while the observation model determines whether missingness concerns individual expression entries or complete spatial profiles. GraphPathology distinguishes itself through complete-vector withholding at selected locations, separate histological and anchor-support pathways, and fixed-target placement comparisons, without using a gene-interaction network as a reconstruction input.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Predicting spatial expression from histological images.}
ST-Net and DeepSpaCE learn relationships between histological appearance and spatial gene expression, establishing image-based prediction as a complementary approach to direct molecular profiling~\cite{he2020,monjo2022}. Contextual models extend this principle: Hist2ST combines convolutional feature extraction, Transformer context and graph-based neighborhood modeling; THItoGene combines richer local representations with global and graph-attention components; and FmH2ST integrates foundation-model representations with spatial and semantic graph information~\cite{hist2st2022,thitogene2024,fmh2st2025}. These methods motivate the study of morphology at multiple contextual scales, but their published results are not an equal-input benchmark for the present sparse-anchor task. CMRCNet also investigates cross-modal reconstruction and contrastive learning for expression prediction~\cite{liu2026}. GraphPathology differs by making observed same-section expression an explicit inference resource and measuring the incremental contribution of image context against an anchor-matched molecular baseline.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Multimodal reconstruction and spatial super-resolution.}
Methods integrating molecular measurements with histology address a range of reconstruction resolutions and information settings. XFuse uses a generative fusion model to infer higher-resolution expression maps, TESLA integrates histology and expression to enhance molecular maps and also evaluates masked-spot recovery, and iStar uses hierarchical image features to predict expression at finer spatial resolution~\cite{bergenstrahle2022,tesla2023,istar2024}. PanoSpace further combines low-resolution ST, high-resolution histology and matched single-cell RNA sequencing for continuous, single-cell-level tissue mapping~\cite{panospace2026}. These methods connect spatial reconstruction to resolution enhancement and multimodal integration, with different assumptions about measured tissue and available molecular references. GraphPathology's distinguishing emphasis is reconstruction at the retained assay locations and controlled separation of anchor geometry, estimator choice and added histological information, rather than increased output resolution or inferred cell identities.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Sparse-measurement learning and acquisition strategies.}
Sparser2Sparse addresses ST imputation through within-sample sparser-to-sparse learning, natural-image co-learning and data-consistent reconstruction~\cite{sparser2026}. SCR$^2$-ST combines single-cell prior information, reinforcement-learning-based active sampling and a regression--retrieval predictor to improve acquisition and prediction under limited budgets~\cite{scr2st2026}. These approaches directly share the motivation of recovering molecular information from restricted measurements. Conventional inverse-distance interpolation also provides an established spatial estimator against which the added value of learned components can be assessed~\cite{shepard1968}. The relevant distinction is therefore not sparse acquisition alone, but the question asked of the acquisition design. GraphPathology complements acquisition-optimized systems by isolating a prespecified anchor-placement effect at fixed measurement count, estimator and hidden targets, rather than proposing an optimized sampling policy.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Benchmarking, generalization and uncertainty.}
HEST-1k provides paired histology--expression resources for evaluating computational representations, while cross-study benchmarking by Wang and colleagues examines prediction quality, generalizability and translational considerations across histology-based expression models~\cite{jaume2024,wang2025}. More broadly, structured validation is important when observations exhibit spatial or hierarchical dependence~\cite{roberts2017}. TISSUE addresses a different aspect of reliability by estimating uncertainty in spatial-expression predictions and incorporating it into downstream analyses~\cite{sun2024}. Patient-level uncertainty in an aggregate performance contrast is not interchangeable with calibrated uncertainty for an individual reconstructed expression value. GraphPathology complements these efforts through patient-resolved, anchor-matched comparisons and identical-target placement controls, reporting reconstruction errors and descriptive patient-bootstrap intervals without claiming calibrated prediction intervals or superiority to the cited systems.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Study design separated information access and dataset roles}
The task used a fixed 50-gene panel and paired spatial expression and histology (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}; Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}). Four HEST patients supplied 35,536 locations: two patients for fitting, one for validation and one for development evaluation. Eight HER2ST patients (3,455 locations) supported transportability and calibration development; their reuse precludes treating them as untouched external validation. The original external evaluation comprised three held-out Wu patients and the first numbered section from each of 22 Bassiouni patients, totaling 25 patients and 38,484 locations. All 50 genes were retained externally; HER2ST used a fixed 40-gene intersection.

The additional placement controls and neural comparisons reused these already evaluated cohorts and one development patient. They are exploratory follow-up analyses, not new blind replication. Image-only predictors receive histology and permitted geometry, molecular estimators receive coordinates and same-section anchor vectors, and fusion or residual predictors receive both. Thus image-only versus molecular comparisons quantify the value of measured anchors under unequal information access; incremental image value requires comparison with the same anchors supplied to both methods. The public external images were downsampled, containing only 15/25 native pixels in Wu and 24--29 in Bassiouni across the nominal 112-\um{} patch.

\subsection{Controlled anchor placement altered reconstruction in all 22 patients}
The controlled experiment fixed a central region $R$ and a hidden subset $H$, then changed only the eligible placement of an equal number of anchors. Distributed anchors could occupy any non-$H$ location; region-excluded anchors were restricted outside $R$. Within each comparison, the estimator, gene panel, hidden targets and total anchor count were identical. This design isolates placement for the specified central-region sampling rule.

At 20\% coverage, region exclusion worsened Pearson, MSE and MAE in all 22 Bassiouni patients for both fixed estimators (Fig.~\ref{fig:extension_placement}; Table~\ref{tab:extension_placement}). For inverse-distance weighting (IDW), the median paired correlation change was $-0.1541$ (descriptive 95\% patient-bootstrap interval $-0.2056$ to $-0.1096$), relative MSE increased 45.0\% (29.8--52.2\%) and MAE increased 0.0335 (0.0266--0.0508). Uniform interpolation gave a correlation change of $-0.1502$ ($-0.1805$ to $-0.1094$), relative MSE increase 39.9\% (29.2--53.5\%) and MAE increase 0.0326 (0.0234--0.0462). All three budgets and all patient effects are retained. These intervals describe uncertainty across the 22 patients; they are not p-values or evidence of a universal geometry effect.

\subsection{Incremental value of graph context across matched visual backbones}
The matched comparisons separated expression prediction from the incremental value of histology when molecular anchors were available. Within each frozen backbone, local and graph heads used the same registered images, targets and evaluation supports, with equal validation-only selection opportunities (Table~\ref{tab:matched_backbone}; Fig.~\ref{fig:matched_backbone_effects}). Patient effects average mask and training seeds before pairing; the three Wu patients and the single development patient remain separate from Bassiouni.

\paragraph{Image-based prediction.}
Graph context did not produce a uniform image-only advantage. In Bassiouni, ResNet18 GCN versus its matched local predictor changed median paired correlation by $+0.0079$, relative MSE by $-12.97$\% and MAE by $-0.0150$; 14/22, 22/22 and 16/22 patients improved, respectively. GraphSAGE reduced MSE and MAE but decreased correlation ($-0.0089$), a tradeoff. With Phikon, GCN produced small median changes ($+0.0007$ correlation, $-0.43$\% MSE and $-0.0002$ MAE), with correlation improved in 11/22 patients and its descriptive interval spanning zero. Phikon GraphSAGE decreased median correlation by 0.0016 and increased relative MSE by 8.21\% and MAE by 0.0023. In Wu, both Phikon graph heads had small positive median correlation changes but worse median errors; the three individual effects are retained in the source tables.

\paragraph{Reconstruction with identical molecular anchors.}
Adding the same observed-residual reconstruction rule changed the comparison. GCN improved correlation, MSE and MAE over its matched local-assisted predictor in all 22 Bassiouni patients for both encoders. ResNet18 median paired changes were $+0.0481$, $-17.84$\% and $-0.0295$, respectively; Phikon changes were smaller, at $+0.0075$, $-1.70$\% and $-0.0045$. The corresponding three endpoint gains also occurred in all three Wu patients. GraphSAGE improved all three endpoints in all 22 Bassiouni patients with ResNet18; with Phikon, MSE improved in 20/22 and MAE in 22/22, while correlation improved in 15/22 and its interval included zero. The common-valid-gene sensitivity gave the same median GCN correlation gains for both backbones. Thus context could improve a local visual reconstruction without yielding a uniform architecture- or endpoint-independent advantage.

\paragraph{Comparison with molecular interpolation.}
Those gains did not establish an advantage over molecular support alone. Against the identical-anchor IDW reference in Bassiouni, residual GCN gave median paired correlation changes of $-0.0297$ with ResNet18 and $-0.0097$ with Phikon, relative MSE increases of 4.64\% and 0.37\%, and MAE increases of 0.0124 and 0.0028. GraphSAGE also worsened all three cohort-median endpoints versus IDW in both backbones. Phikon GCN in Wu slightly improved median correlation and MSE versus IDW, but MAE worsened in all three patients. Such results are error tradeoffs, not joint superiority. Development-patient improvements against interpolation therefore remain development-specific evidence rather than consistent external gains.

\paragraph{Aggregation and spatial controls.}
Neither graph head improved the three cohort-median endpoints over the neighborhood-mean control in either backbone. Spatial GraphSAGE outperformed its shuffled control on all three median endpoints in both encoders, whereas the ResNet18 GCN shuffled-control comparison traded higher correlation for worse errors. These comparisons use independently validation-selected heads and do not isolate an inference-only edge intervention. Taken together, the controls support testing neighboring image information, but do not establish an advantage of the tested graph normalization or concatenation over simple neighborhood averaging. Complete controls, alternative coverage and placement conditions, undefined-gene counts and common-valid-gene sensitivities remain available in the machine-readable results.

\subsection{Nonlocal and latent-coordinate updates did not improve molecular interpolation}

The full-section architectural comparison retained all 48 fits alongside the 30 frozen minibatch fits in TENX99, Wu 3 and Bassiouni 22, using identical masks and information routes. Architectural definitions and permitted inputs are specified in Methods. In the primary 20\% distributed Bassiouni comparison, the residual-anchor GraphGPS-inspired model changed paired patient correlation versus IDW by $-0.0504$ (95\% interval $-0.0629$ to $-0.0389$), relative MSE by $+13.4$\% (11.3--18.4\%) and MAE by $+0.0244$ (0.0204--0.0295). The latent-coordinate EGNN gave $-0.0480$ ($-0.0698$ to $-0.0368$), $+13.0$\% (9.1--18.3\%) and $+0.0252$ (0.0188--0.0310), respectively. Neither primary model improved any of these endpoints in any of the 22 patients. Under identical-$H$ region exclusion, their median relative MSE increases were 10.2\% and 9.2\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:extension_neural}).

Component comparisons also failed to establish the proposed gains: the full nonlocal model worsened correlation and errors relative to its local counterpart, and latent-coordinate updates did not improve the fixed-distance model (Table~\ref{tab:extension_controls}). Same-information conventional neural comparisons, all routes, individual Wu effects, training seeds and distance-bin support are retained in Supplementary Figs.~S15--S25,S28 and Tables~S14--S17. Conditional geometric and reach checks confirm the implemented properties; they do not establish biological long-range association or full-image equivariance. These negative findings concern the tested training budget, frozen features and low-resolution cross-cohort inputs.

\subsection{Regional completion, distance and calibration defined failure regimes}
The original contiguous-completion protocol was substantially harder than distributed reconstruction. At 20\% coverage, Bassiouni correlation fell from 0.416 to 0.171, MSE increased from 0.0923 to 0.1496 and MAE from 0.2033 to 0.2624. Every patient deteriorated; median paired correlation change was $-0.267$ and relative MSE increase 52.8\%. Wu correlation fell from 0.359 to 0.0815, with lower correlation and higher errors in every patient (Supplementary Table~S9). Because the interpolation rule and scored tissue also change, these effects must not be attributed solely to geometry. A withheld region may still lie inside the anchors' convex hull.

Distance-resolved errors were heterogeneous (Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}). At 20\% Bassiouni coverage, farthest-bin MSE exceeded nearest-bin MSE in 15/22 patients under distributed interpolation and 20/22 under regional completion; strictly increasing curves occurred in only 6 and 13 patients. Wu distributed curves increased across occupied bins, while regional curves were nonmonotonic. Bin support and tissue composition differ, precluding a universal reliability cutoff (Supplementary Table~S7).

\section{Discussion}
Measurement placement was a determinant of reconstruction quality even when the amount of observed expression was held fixed. The 22-patient comparison shows that equal budgets can produce different performance when anchors are excluded from the region containing the scored targets. Its strength is the fixed hidden set and estimator, which separate placement from changes in tissue support or reconstruction rule. The result applies to the specified central-region design; it does not establish a universally optimal sampling policy or biological correlation length.

Distributed molecular anchors nevertheless supported useful reconstruction of the predefined panel across two breast-cancer studies. The coverage response suggests that nearby same-section measurements carry information that a transported image predictor may not recover. This is a task-specific advantage of measured expression, not an equal-information demonstration that interpolation outperforms all image-based systems. Reliability should be assessed on the intended acquisition design: favorable distributed masking does not ensure accurate completion of a large unmeasured region~\cite{roberts2017}.

The matched-backbone results refine the role of graph context. With identical molecular anchors, a GCN improved on its matched local visual predictor across both encoders in all 22 Bassiouni patients, although the magnitude depended strongly on the representation. This is a benefit relative to a non-graph visual reconstruction, not evidence of superiority to molecular interpolation. Image-only gains were less consistent, and some correlation improvements accompanied worse errors. Neighborhood averaging remained a strong control, preventing attribution of the observed benefit specifically to the tested graph operators.

The broader architectural comparisons retain an important negative finding: nonlocal access and latent-coordinate updates did not establish the proposed external advantage over the same molecular reference. Functional attention reach and conditional equivariance are software properties, rather than evidence of learned molecular relationships. Together, the two comparisons distinguish useful context relative to a local predictor from incremental value beyond measured expression, and support retaining strong molecular and aggregation baselines.

Cross-cohort histology remains a substantial limitation. Public images were heavily downsampled, encoders were frozen, and fitting used only two source patients. Cross-backbone differences include pretraining, feature dimension and effective physical field of view; they cannot be attributed solely to adding graphs. The frozen transported calibration was shared within comparisons rather than re-estimated for each model on evaluation labels. Differences in capture depth, support and composition remain after mathematical normalization. Calibration can change image predictions, but cannot create a new interpolation benefit when a per-gene affine transform is inverted around the same normalized weighted average. Native-resolution representations, broader source training and genuinely independent evaluation would be needed to establish whether morphology adds reproducible incremental information.

The scope is also limited by retrospective masking, a development-selected 50-gene panel (40 genes in HER2ST), small Wu patient numbers and restriction to breast tissue. The exploratory controls reused evaluated cohorts. Genes, sites and repeated masks do not increase biological sample size, and descriptive patient intervals do not establish clinical generalizability. Distance alone did not define a reliable error threshold; occupied-bin support and patient-specific failures should remain visible before reconstructed expression is used downstream. Approaches such as TISSUE motivate explicit uncertainty assessment~\cite{sun2024}, but calibrated predictive uncertainty was not evaluated here.

Coverage is a count of measured locations, not a measured financial saving. If a full assay has fixed cost $C_0$ and per-location cost $c$ for $N$ locations, the conditional ratio is
\begin{equation}
\frac{C_{\mathrm{full}}}{C_{20\%}}=\frac{C_0+cN}{C_0+0.2cN}\leq5.
\label{eq:cost_ratio}
\end{equation}
Only a purely location-dependent component could fall fivefold at 20\% coverage. Selective capture, laboratory expenditure, sequencing requirements and downstream accuracy were not prospectively tested. Future studies could selectively profile a subset of tissue locations and reconstruct the predefined expression panel at the remaining locations, while evaluating placement, reconstruction error and realized costs prospectively.

GraphPathology therefore supports an information-explicit account of sparse reconstruction: measured molecular support can be useful, its placement matters under controlled conditions, and contextual gains over local predictors must be distinguished from improvement over the same molecular support. Prospective sampling and independent native-resolution cohorts are the next tests of practical value.
\section{Methods}
\subsection{Data, target panel and preprocessing}
All experiments used previously released paired spatial expression and histology data. The four development samples were obtained through HEST~\cite{jaume2024}: NCBI783 and NCBI785 were the training patients, TENX95 was the validation patient and TENX99 was the primary development evaluation patient. The cohort comprised 7,015 training locations, 7,760 validation locations and 20,761 primary evaluation locations. Earlier conditional leave-one-patient-out analyses reused frozen gene panels and development-selected configurations and were interpreted as sensitivity analyses, not unbiased external cross-validation.

HER2ST~\cite{andersson2021} contributed one patient-specific section for each of A1--H1. Initial evaluation was followed by patient-disjoint calibration development using the same inspected cohort; these two analytical roles are distinguished throughout. In the Wu local Visium cohort~\cite{wu2021}, patients 1142243F, CID4465 and CID4535 supplied calibration statistics, and patients 1160920F, CID4290 and CID44971 remained held out. The Bassiouni evaluation cohort was obtained from GSE210616~\cite{bassiouni2023}. The source study includes 43 sections from 22 patients. We selected the first numbered section for every published patient from metadata before target evaluation, yielding BASS\_P01--P09, P11--P15 and P17--P24. Gaps in identifiers reflect source numbering rather than outcome-dependent exclusion. The source study's own reference and validation groups were not used as calibration groups in this reconstruction study.

Metadata, repository identifiers, prior-use records and available asset hashes were checked to exclude reused or reprocessed development samples. Such checks support cohort independence but cannot conclusively establish patient identity across unrelated anonymized repositories. Cohort roles and sample counts are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}; identifiers and exact panels are supplied in Supplementary Tables~S1 and S2.

The primary panel contained the 50 genes ranked using development-training expression variance; ranking did not use evaluation expression. The development assay provided 280 harmonized biological genes, and larger 250- and 280-gene settings were used in earlier development analyses. The original external studies retained all 50 genes. HER2ST retained the frozen 40-gene intersection without replacing missing genes. The optional 278/280 intersection in the Bassiouni metadata was not used to claim a secondary whole-transcriptome experiment.

For the second replication, the source filtered feature-barcode H5 files, tissue positions, scale-factor JSON files and tissue images were acquired for each selected section. Barcode joins and published in-tissue flags defined included locations. No new expression-derived quality threshold, tissue-class annotation or deconvolution output was used to select evaluation locations. Counts were checked for finite, nonnegative integer values. For count $c_{ig}$ at location $i$ and gene $g$, the evaluation target was
\begin{equation}
 y_{ig}=\frac{\log(1+c_{ig})-\mu_g^{\mathrm{dev}}}{\sigma_g^{\mathrm{dev}}},
 \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
where the centering and scaling constants were the pooled location mean and population standard deviation frozen from development-training patients; a standard deviation below $10^{-8}$ was replaced by 1. No destination library-size or capture-area correction was fitted. Identical target units were used for all methods within each comparison. These common mathematical units do not imply cross-platform calibration of absolute molecular abundance. Supplementary Fig.~S2 summarizes cross-cohort patterns without pooling panels; the gene-level results are provided in Supplementary Table~S3.

\subsection{Registration and frozen image features}\label{sec:registration}
Visium array coordinates were mapped to image coordinates by a least-squares fit to the source hexagonal lattice with 100-\um{} spacing. Coordinate residuals, image scale and anisotropy were checked against the frozen qualification settings, and overlays were inspected before outcome evaluation. These tests concern location consistency and do not establish cell-level registration. Images were not super-resolved.

The Wu and Bassiouni images contained only 15/25 and 24--29 native pixels, respectively, across the nominal 112-\um{} patch width. Resizing does not recover morphology absent from these public inputs. Image quality was therefore recorded as a limitation of the image comparator, not as a restriction on spatial interpolation that uses only coordinates and observed expression.

At each external location, an RGB patch covered a nominal $112$-\um{} square, rounded to the available native pixels. The patch was resized with LANCZOS to $224\times224$ pixels. The torchvision \texttt{IMAGENET1K\_V1} preprocessing then resized the shorter side to 256 pixels using bilinear interpolation, took a central $224\times224$ crop, rescaled intensities to $[0,1]$, and normalized RGB channels using means $(0.485, 0.456, 0.406)$ and standard deviations $(0.229, 0.224, 0.225)$. The second resize and central crop were retained explicitly; $112$~\um{} denotes the nominal extracted patch, rather than the final uncropped field seen by the network. Development embeddings used the supplied $224\times224$ HEST patches with the same torchvision transform. No stain normalization, random image augmentation or encoder fine-tuning was applied.

The frozen ResNet18~\cite{he2016} comprised a $7\times7$ convolutional stem with stride 2, batch normalization, a rectified-linear activation and $3\times3$ max pooling with stride 2, followed by four residual stages. The stages contained two basic blocks each, with channel widths 64, 128, 256 and 512. Each block used two $3\times3$ convolutions with batch normalization and a rectified-linear activation after the first convolution and after shortcut addition. Identity or projected shortcuts matched the feature dimensions. Global average pooling produced a 512-dimensional vector; the original ImageNet classifier was replaced by the identity operation. All encoder parameters and batch-normalization statistics remained fixed during feature extraction.

The final central crop retains approximately 0.875 of the extracted patch linear field.

For the matched-backbone comparison, we used the frozen Phikon ViT-B/16 histopathology encoder and its published preprocessing pipeline~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit,filiot2023}. Its official ViTImageProcessor consumes the same registered RGB patches, resizes to $224\times224$, rescales by $1/255$ and uses ImageNet channel means and standard deviations, without augmentation or stain normalization. The 12-layer encoder preserves the full patch field and returns the 768-dimensional final-layer-normalized classification token without a pooler. ViT attention relates tokens within a patch; the tissue graph adds relationships between registered locations. The ResNet18 transform retains $224/256=0.875$ of each patch width after resizing and center cropping; its effective field therefore differs from Phikon's. Within each backbone, all matched heads use identical input and embedding bytes. Cross-backbone differences include field of view, pretraining and representation dimension and are not attributed solely to architecture class. Phikon extraction used float32 scaled dot-product attention with four concurrent eight-patch forwards, one CPU intra-operation thread per forward, and no encoder gradient computation. Source-only resource benchmarks and exact serial/concurrent checks preceded extraction; interrupted partial caches and resource revisions were retained.

\subsection{Coordinate-defined graph representations}
\label{sec:graph_methods}
Each retained location is a node with physical position $q_i\in\mathbb R^2$ in micrometers and a frozen image embedding. Following message-passing principles~\cite{gilmer2017}, graphs were constructed independently within each section from physical coordinates, without expression labels. The candidates were symmetrized six-nearest-neighbor adjacency capped at 250~\um{}, radius graphs at 150 or 250~\um{}, and Delaunay adjacency capped at 250~\um{}. Symmetrization takes the union of directed nearest-neighbor edges, so final degree need not equal six. The binary adjacency $A$ had no self edges. With $\widetilde A=A+I$, degree matrix $\widetilde D$ of $\widetilde A$, and degree matrix $D$ of $A$, the three fixed operators were
\[
 P=\widetilde D^{-1}\widetilde A,\qquad
 Q=\widetilde D^{-1/2}\widetilde A\widetilde D^{-1/2},\qquad
 R=D^{-1}A.
\]
For $R$, an isolated node has a zero neighbor aggregate. The input to the MLP was $PX$ for mean pooling, $QX$ for GCN and $[X,RX]$ for mean-GraphSAGE, where $X$ contains standardized frozen image embeddings and brackets denote concatenation. Each predictor therefore applies one fixed graph aggregation followed by the one-hidden-layer MLP with the regime-specific dimensions and scaling specified below. These single-step predictors used neither learned adjacency nor attention; the additional message-passing models are defined below.

Graph choice was selected at seed 0 by the highest validation median gene Pearson correlation, then lower MSE and deterministic graph-name order. All three 50-gene predictors selected capped Delaunay adjacency. Mean pooling and GCN used width 128; GraphSAGE used width 67 to approximately match the initial local head's parameter count after doubling the input dimension. With ResNet18, these models contain 72,114 and 72,075 trainable head parameters, respectively; all use dropout 0.1. Supplementary Table~S8 summarizes the selected dimensions.


\subsection{Neural predictors and distinct training regimes}
\label{sec:experiments}
All neural fitting used NCBI783/NCBI785 and validation-only stopping on TENX95. The following regimes are distinct; parameter similarity does not imply identical update schedules or effective capacity.

\paragraph{Original primary image ensemble.}
Each expression head was a one-hidden-layer MLP with the exact sequence
\begin{equation}
512 \xrightarrow{\mathrm{linear}}256
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{GELU}}256
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{dropout}\;(0.1)}256
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{linear}}50,
\label{eq:head}
\end{equation}
where GELU denotes the Gaussian error linear unit. Both linear layers included biases and used the default PyTorch initialization, uniform on $[-1/\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{in}}},1/\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{in}}}]$. The output was linear, with no nonnegativity constraint. Each head contained 144,178 trainable parameters. Feature-wise centering and scaling, $S_x$, were fitted to the pooled 7,015 training locations from NCBI783 and NCBI785. A separate per-gene scaler, $S_y$, was fitted to their already normalized expression targets. Both used population standard deviations, replacing scales below $10^{-8}$ with 1. If $f_{\theta_s}$ denotes the head trained with seed $s\in\{0,1,2\}$ and $\phi_{\mathrm{R18}}$ the frozen encoder, the image prediction in the common target units was
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\boldsymbol y}^{\mathrm{image}}_i
=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{s=0}^{2}
S_y^{-1}\!\left[f_{\theta_s}\!\left(S_x\!\left(\phi_{\mathrm{R18}}(x_i)\right)\right)\right].
\label{eq:image_ensemble}
\end{equation}
Dropout was disabled at inference. The three inverse-scaled predictions were averaged without fitting ensemble weights.

Heads minimized mean squared error in the additionally standardized target space using AdamW, learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9, 0.999)$ and $\epsilon=10^{-8}$. Weight decay applied to all head parameters, including biases. Each epoch shuffled the training locations into minibatches of 512, retaining the final partial batch, for at most 80 epochs. Training used CPU float32 computation and no learning-rate scheduler. Early stopping used the TENX95 validation patient: an improvement required validation MSE to decrease by more than $10^{-4}$, and training stopped after 10 consecutive epochs without such an improvement. The best qualifying parameters were restored. Hidden widths $\{128,256\}$ and dropout probabilities $\{0, 0.1\}$ were compared at seed 0; the configuration with the highest validation median gene Pearson correlation was selected, with lower validation MSE breaking ties. This configuration-selection criterion was distinct from the within-fit stopping criterion. The selected width-256, dropout-0.1 heads were fitted with seeds 0, 1 and 2; their retained epochs were 12, 11 and 12, respectively. No external patient was used to select or refit these heads.


\paragraph{Additional minibatch heads.}
Thirty additional local, mean, GCN and GraphSAGE heads (three seeds per specified configuration/control) used the following settings. They were fitted separately from the original primary ensemble and were later frozen as references.
A single learned linear/GELU/dropout/readout layer followed mean or symmetric GCN aggregation; GraphSAGE concatenated self and neighbor mean. Widths were 128 for local/mean/GCN and 67 for SAGE (72,114 versus 72,075 parameters). Spatial, self-only and degree-preserving node-shuffled controls shared each family's nominal parameters, labels and training settings. SAGE self-only concatenated X with X and has a smaller effective function class. AdamW used lr $10^{-3}$, decay $10^{-4}$, batch size 512, dropout 0.1, maximum 80 epochs, patience 10 and minimum validation improvement $10^{-4}$; TENX95 alone selected stopping. Seeds 0/1/2 were all retained. Deterministic float32 CPU execution used four torch/BLAS threads; Local, mean-self and GCN-self identity controls used the same inputs. Their float32 weight/prediction comparisons allowed rtol=atol=$32\epsilon_{\mathrm{float32}}=3.8147\times10^{-6}$; this tolerance did not relax float64 molecular replay or exact hidden-label invariance. Further identity diagnostics are in Supplementary Methods.

The matched-backbone follow-up reused the local, mean, GCN, GraphSAGE and shuffled GCN/GraphSAGE configurations from this minibatch regime (three seeds each), retaining the remaining historical controls separately. Phikon used local/mean/GCN width 128 and GraphSAGE width 66, giving 104,882 and 104,792 parameters; the within-backbone capacity mismatch was below 0.1\%. Approximate parameter matching does not equalize hidden width, functional class or inference cost. Separate population feature scalers were fitted on NCBI783/NCBI785 for each encoder; both used common C0 targets without another head-target scaler. The encoders stayed frozen.

A documented validation-guided refinement followed the initial Phikon fits, 17 of which selected the first epoch while later fitting loss declined and validation error rose. Before any Phikon evaluation outcomes, every backbone/configuration was assigned the same two learning-rate candidates, $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$, with the minibatch and stopping rules above. Eighteen existing ResNet18 fits and 18 initial Phikon fits supplied the first candidate; 36 additional fits supplied the smaller rate. One rate per backbone/configuration was selected by mean best qualifying TENX95 MSE across seeds 0/1/2; exact ties favored the smaller rate. All three seeds at that rate entered the primary evaluation. All 72 candidate states, histories and validation losses were preserved, including unselected trials. No test outcome selected rates, architectures, seeds or fusion. This adaptive source/validation refinement is exploratory follow-up, not formal preregistration. Selected/run epochs, actual update counts, input/state hashes and observed costs appear in Supplementary Table~S18 and the manifests. No new state replaces a missing historical binary.

\paragraph{Full-section comparison.}
The 48 additional fits crossed 16 configurations with seeds 0/1/2. AdamW used learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, maximum 80 epochs, patience 10 and minimum validation-MSE improvement $10^{-4}$. Each epoch made one full-section update per source training patient in seeded random order. Local/mean/GCN widths were 128 and concatenated GraphSAGE width 67. These baselines match the new models' update budget, whereas the 30 frozen minibatch heads do not. Both additional regimes trained directly in the common development target units, without the primary ensemble's extra head-target scaler. Model selection retained every seed; TENX99 was used only for evaluation. Parameter counts, selected epochs, histories and computational costs are in Supplementary Tables S15 and S16. Initial development searches, Phikon representation comparisons~\cite{filiot2023} and conditional leave-one-patient-out fits have separate specifications in Supplementary Methods S6.

\subsection{Bounded nonlocal and distance-aware message passing}
This implementation draws on GraphGPS principles rather than reproducing the reference implementation. Its blocks use a $512$-to-$64$ projection, two 64-wide blocks, four attention heads, dropout 0.1, pre-LayerNorm, local linear messages gated by eight relative-distance radial features, parallel bounded attention, residual additions and a two-layer GELU feed-forward path. The local graph is Delaunay with a $250$\,\um{} cap. Global keys/values are at most 32 deterministic coordinate-only farthest-point landmarks, giving $O(NL)$ attention storage with $L\leq32$; there is no dense N-by-N allocation. The nonlocal-disabled model attends self plus up to seven nearest graph neighbors, padding unused slots with self. Thus full/local changes attention range and key count as well as connectivity, despite identical 121,018 nominal parameters. Local radial features use log(1+d/250) with eight centers 0--1 and width 0.15; attention relative bias uses eight centers 0--4 and width 0.5. The distance-disabled version removes explicit gates/biases but retains coordinate-defined graph/landmark selection and is not coordinate-free.

The invariant fixed-coordinate and EGNN alternatives use two 64-wide message blocks. With centered normalized $p_i=(q_i-\overline q)/250$, where $q_i$ is the physical position in micrometers, $m_{ij}=\phi_e(h_i,h_j,\mathrm{RBF}(\|p_i-p_j\|^2))$, node updates aggregate the mean message through $\phi_h$, using SiLU, LayerNorm and dropout 0.1. Eight radial centers span 0--4 with width 0.5. EGNN additionally updates private coordinates as
\[
p_i^{l+1}=p_i^l+\frac{1}{\max(1,|\mathcal N_i|)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}0.1\tanh\!\big(\phi_x(m_{ij})\big)(p_i^l-p_j^l).
\]
Each $\phi_x$ is a bias-free 64-to-1 map initialized at standard deviation 0.001. Only the first coordinate update affects the second layer's scalar readout; the last update has no downstream scalar loss. EGNN has 87,282 nominal parameters versus 87,154 for fixed distance (128 additional coordinate coefficients, of which the last 64 do not affect prediction). Both are E(2)-compatible conditional on frozen features, so their accuracy difference cannot identify equivariance itself. Latent positions never change original masks, anchors or IDW distances.

Self-only and coordinate/graph-shuffled variants retain nominal parameter tensors but have different effective function classes; inactive attention or edge parameters do not make them capacity-equivalent. Conjugating the graph and assigned coordinates together preserves edge-length geometry while breaking image-to-location assignment. Degree/length rewiring preserves each node's degree and global 50\,\um{} edge-length bins below the same 250\,\um{} cap, targeting $E/4$ accepted double swaps with at most $20E$ attempts. Actual successes are reported, including infeasible or weak rewiring. No hidden expression enters any graph, landmark or distance encoding.


\subsection{Observation masks and controlled placement}
For a section with $N$ retained locations, the observed set $\mathcal O$ contained $\lfloor Nb+0.5\rfloor$ anchors at budget $b\in\{0.10, 0.20, 0.40\}$. Every hidden location lacked its entire expression vector; coordinates and image features remained available. Primary random trajectories used seeds 0--9 with nested prefixes, so higher budgets added anchors without discarding earlier observations. All trajectories were frozen without using expression values.

In distributed interpolation (V1), anchors were eligible throughout the tissue and all unobserved locations were scored. In contiguous regional completion (V2), a forbidden region consisted of the nearest 40\% of locations to a center determined from the 25th, 50th or 75th percentile of the first coordinate principal-component rank. The three centers defined three fixed geometries. Anchors were sampled outside the forbidden region, and only the forbidden region was scored. All other unobserved labels were also removed from model inputs. Thus the same nominal budget refers to the full retained section even when evaluation is restricted to a region.

A deterministic spatial farthest-point policy was retained as a secondary sampling analysis. It started from the eligible location farthest from the eligible-coordinate centroid and selected locations without expression labels. It was not optimized on external outcomes. Earlier development block and capture-window masks differ from the final regional-completion definition and are labeled separately in Fig.~\ref{fig:development} and the source tables.

The controlled identical-target placement experiment (B2) used only the predeclared central PCA-coordinate region $R$ (40\% of locations). $H$ was a fixed random 20\% subset of $R$ (about 8\% of all locations), selected using SHA 256(``H|patient'') first eight bytes, little-endian. Distributed anchors were drawn from all non-H sites; region-exclusion anchors from outside R. Seeds 0--9 gave nested prefixes with equal total counts at every budget. R, H, barcodes and anchor trajectories were persisted before scoring. Both estimators scored exactly H; no expression-based geometry or exclusion was used. Seed means preceded geometry means and patient pairing. Both primary error and correlation effects and nearest-anchor distances were exported, with occupied-bin errors and support.


The additional neural matrix used three original random masks per budget for TENX99, and original V1/central-V2 geometry 1 masks with seeds 0/1/2 externally. All 78 fits also used B2 seeds 0/1/2 at 10/20/40\% budgets. The matched-backbone study used these exact same mask and scoring supports, retaining their hashes. This three-mask subset is distinct from the ten-seed, three-geometry molecular baseline analysis.

\subsection{Molecular interpolation, calibration and residual prediction}
For every unmeasured location $i\in\mathcal U$, we define directed support edges $i\rightarrow j$ to at most 16 measured locations $j\in\mathcal O$, identified by the smallest coordinate distance. The resulting observation-dependent edge-weight matrix $W_{\mathcal U\mathcal O}$ has row-normalized positive weights and zero entries for unavailable anchors. The molecular reconstruction operator is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{ST}}_{\mathcal U}=W_{\mathcal U\mathcal O}Y_{\mathcal O}.
\label{eq:molecular_graph}
\end{equation}
The operator has no explicit maximum-distance cutoff, permitting cross-gap transport of observed expression. It is not a learned attention matrix, and absence of a cutoff is not evidence of reliable long-range inference. The distinct V1 and V2 weights and scoring supports are defined next.

The molecular predictor used the up to 16 nearest observed anchors, $\mathcal N_{16}(i;\mathcal O)$. For distinct observed and hidden coordinates, distributed interpolation used normalized inverse-distance-squared weights, a local form of inverse-distance interpolation~\cite{shepard1968}:
\begin{equation}
 \widehat y_{ig}^{\mathrm{ST}}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_{16}(i;\mathcal O)}w_{ij}y_{jg},\qquad
 w_{ij}=\frac{\|q_i-q_j\|^{-2}}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal N_{16}(i;\mathcal O)}\|q_i-q_k\|^{-2}}.
 \label{eq:interp}
\end{equation}
For the frozen V2 protocol, the same neighborhood size was used with equal weights $w_{ij}=1/|\mathcal N_{16}|$. These rules were fixed during the Wu evaluation and were not selected on Bassiouni outcomes. Fewer than 16 anchors, if available, were handled by using all available anchors. Euclidean distances were rounded to six decimal places in micrometers and floored at $10^{-6}$~\um{} before inverse-square weighting; the normalized weights therefore remained finite at coincident coordinates.

The image-only prediction was denoted $\widehat y^{\mathrm{image}}$. The final fusion was
\begin{equation}
 \widehat y_{ig}^{\mathrm{fusion}}=0.8\widehat y_{ig}^{\mathrm{ST}}+
 0.2\widetilde y_{ig}^{\mathrm{image}},
 \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
where the image calibration was
\begin{equation}
\widetilde y^{\mathrm{image}}_{ig}
=a^{\mathrm W}_g+b^{\mathrm W}_g
\frac{\widehat y^{\mathrm{image}}_{ig}-a^{\mathrm S}_g}{b^{\mathrm S}_g}.
\label{eq:calibration}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathrm S$ denotes the two development-training patients and $\mathrm W$ the three Wu calibration patients. Moments were computed from all retained targets of the designated source and calibration patients, rather than their sparse anchor subsets or image predictions. Each patient contributed equal total weight: for a domain with $P$ patients and $n_p$ locations in patient $p$,
\begin{equation}
 a_g=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\frac{1}{n_p}\sum_{i=1}^{n_p}y_{pig},
 \qquad
 b_g=\sqrt{\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\frac{1}{n_p}\sum_{i=1}^{n_p}(y_{pig}-a_g)^2}.
\label{eq:moments}
\end{equation}
A scale below $10^{-8}$ was replaced by 1. This scale includes variation about the shared domain center; it is not the mean of patient-specific standard deviations. All moments used the common target units in Eq.~\ref{eq:target}. Bassiouni evaluation reused these source and Wu constants without fitting new moments or fusion weights. The calibrated image term used in fusion therefore differs from the raw image-only comparator reported in Table~\ref{tab:performance}.

Per-gene affine transformation, normalized interpolation and inverse transformation are algebraically equivalent to interpolation in the original units (Supplementary Note S1). Calibration therefore changes the image term, not the underlying normalized molecular operator.

For additional graph comparisons, $g_{ig}$ denotes the gene-$g$ image prediction at site $i$ in the common target space, calibrated by Eq.~\ref{eq:calibration} for both external cohorts. Alongside image-only $g$ and fixed fusion, we evaluated observed-residual interpolation,
\begin{equation}
\widehat y_{ig}=g_{ig}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal O}w_{ij}\,[y_{jg}-g_{jg}].
\label{eq:observed_residual}
\end{equation}
Only observed residual vectors enter this predictor; no regression coefficients are learned from the evaluation section. It is distinct from the development ridge residual correction, which fits gene-specific coefficients to image and molecular graph summaries using training patients (Supplementary Methods S6). Neither should be interchanged with fixed image fusion. HER2ST patient-disjoint calibration and fusion-family selection are also specified in Supplementary Methods S6; the final external image weight and Wu-derived moments remained fixed.

The matched-estimator grid used all ten original random V1 trajectories at each of 10/20/40\% coverage for nearest anchor; uniform k=4,8,16,32; IDW at those k with p=1,2,3; and Gaussian k=8,16,32. We retained cKDTree ordering, six-decimal distances and a $10^{-6}$~\um{} inverse-weight floor. Gaussian bandwidth was the median observed-anchor distance to its kth other observed neighbor, with k limited by available anchors; it never used hidden expression. Every declared estimator is reported. The operator-by-protocol comparison (B1) held each original mask and scored set fixed while changing IDW k16/p2 versus uniform k16, for both V1 and all three V2 geometries.


\subsection{Metrics, patient effects and uncertainty}
All expression metrics were computed on hidden locations only; the full observed expression vector was available at each anchor and the full target vector was withheld at every other location. Observed rows were selected before molecular prediction, so hidden expression could not influence anchor values or interpolation weights. For each gene, Pearson and Spearman correlations summarized spatial association, while MSE and MAE summarized prediction error. Panel Pearson values were medians across genes with defined correlations. Constant-target or constant-prediction correlations were left undefined rather than assigned zero; such genes did not pass positive-correlation threshold counts. Panel MSE and MAE were means of per-gene errors. In formulae, for the scored hidden set $\mathcal H$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{MSE}=\frac{1}{G|\mathcal H|}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\sum_{i\in\mathcal H}(\widehat y_{ig}-y_{ig})^2,\qquad
 \mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{G|\mathcal H|}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\sum_{i\in\mathcal H}|\widehat y_{ig}-y_{ig}|.
 \label{eq:errors}
\end{equation}
Within each patient and geometry, metrics were averaged over mask seeds; geometry means were then averaged equally. Headline cohort values were medians of these patient summaries. Tables label an MSE value as a cohort median of patient mean MSEs, not a pooled mean over all spots. Paired effects were calculated within patients before taking their median. This distinction is necessary because a median paired effect need not equal the difference or ratio of marginal medians.

The patient was the biological unit. Seeds described computational or mask variability, not additional patients.  The original external-evaluation support criterion required a median gene Pearson gain of at least 0.02 over image-only prediction, relative mean MSE reduction of at least 2\%, and non-worsening MAE. The cohort rule required at least $\lceil2N/3\rceil$ patients meeting the joint criterion at at least two budgets, positive median benefits at every budget, and the same support threshold for correlation/MSE improvement from 10\% to 40\%. For 22 patients this threshold was 15. The limited native image resolution restricts interpretation of the image comparisons even when the molecular support criterion is satisfied.

For the additional neural comparisons, mask means were formed within geometry, geometry means within patient and training seeds averaged within patient before paired effects. Relative MSE is the candidate-minus-reference patient mean MSE divided by the reference mean MSE after these averages. Bassiouni intervals resampled 22 paired patients 10,000 times (seed 20261101) and took percentile 95\% limits for the median effect. They are descriptive, unadjusted intervals, not p-values or simultaneous bands. Cohorts were never pooled; Wu effects are individual, and no population interval was inferred from TENX99. Raw Spearman was secondary because near-tied ranks are sensitive to floating-point differences. Full defined-correlation counts accompany the panel summaries.

For each hidden location, the nearest-anchor distance was $d_i=\min_{j\in\mathcal O}\|q_i-q_j\|$. Distances were grouped into the frozen bins $[0,150)$, $[150,300)$, $[300,600)$, $[600,1200)$, $[1200,2400)$ and $[2400,\infty)$~\um{}. Error summaries were reported for occupied bins; the Bassiouni binwise correlations required at least 20 scored locations, while the Wu distance summaries contained errors only. Empty bins were omitted, not filled with zero. Counts, geometry and run identities were retained to distinguish sparse support from low error. Nearest-versus-farthest contrasts and monotonicity summaries were descriptive properties of the occupied-bin curves, not estimates of a universal biological length scale.

For graph-versus-local and anchor-reference contrasts, the primary Pearson summary retained each run's defined-gene median. A separately labelled sensitivity analysis intersected the finite Pearson genes of the paired methods within each mask/training-seed run, computed both medians on that common set, and applied the same geometry, mask and training-seed averaging before patient pairing. Undefined and common-valid gene counts were exported; undefined correlations were never replaced by zero. Correlation gains with worse MSE or MAE were reported as tradeoffs.

\subsection{Controls and reproducibility}
In the second replication, control patient BASS\_P01, 20\% coverage, random-mask seed 0, V1 geometry 0 and V2 geometry 1 were designated before evaluation. Three control seeds permuted observed expression-vector assignments, anchor-position assignments or image predictions. The first two disrupt related molecular--location associations; they were not interpreted as independent biological evidence. Every saved prediction was also repeated after all unobserved expression was replaced with NaNs and with independent random values. Observed/hidden barcodes, mask identities, configurations and hashes accompanied the predictions.

Analyses used Python, NumPy~\cite{harris2020}, SciPy~\cite{virtanen2020}, pandas, PyTorch and related scientific libraries. Reproduction instructions and environment specifications are maintained with the code. Model training and original external evaluation used recorded configurations and checksums; source-data tables provide the numerical outputs underlying the published figures.

Graphs, anchor trajectories, feature scalers and landmark selection were label-free or fitted on the designated source patients only. Replacing every unobserved label, including unscored labels, with NaNs and independent random values had to leave fixed/residual predictions exactly unchanged. Image-only B2 metrics were also identical across placements and budgets because $H$ was fixed. Input identities and barcode/coordinate joins were checked against original records; independently derived coordinates agreed with preserved label-free coordinates within $10^{-8}$\,\um{}, which governed exact neighbor ties. Numerical source tables, environment records and fitted-state hashes are retained in the repository; the detailed audit counts are summarized in the supplementary source guide.

The original external results were frozen before the additional controls. The latter followed timestamped local protocols, retained all specified configurations/seeds and reused previously inspected patients; they were exploratory, not formally preregistered. No evaluation outcome selected a new architecture, seed, calibration map or fusion coefficient. Original primary heads and newly fitted states have separate identities and availability records.

Conditional geometric tests applied rotations, reflections, translations and node permutations at float64/float32, recording scalar and physical latent errors separately. Float 64 graph-model tolerances were $10^{-8}$; float32 and preaggregated baseline tolerances were $10^{-4}$. Physical latent tolerances included the 250-\um{} normalization. Recomputed-graph tests used nondegenerate coordinates without distance ties. These tests condition on frozen image embeddings and documented graph/landmark handling, not invariance of the complete image pipeline. Attention diagnostics recorded physical/assigned connection fractions and actual mass beyond 250/600/1200\,\um{} per head/layer/seed/patient. A coordinate-selected remote landmark feature was zeroed to test functional dependency, not biological correlation. The degree/length control accepted no swaps in 64/66 recovered-cohort patient--seed graphs, with at most one swap in the remaining graphs; those cases supply little independent comparison.
